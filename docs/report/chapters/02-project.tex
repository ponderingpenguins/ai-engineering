\section{Project Formulation}

\subsection{Problem Formulation and Definition}
In our company meetings, Valtes, our primary stakeholder and available for direct consultation, described its work with mmWave data as limited and exploratory. Its expectation is therefore an exploratory analysis of what can be measured, detected, and inferred from its device's recordings and how this information could support at-home care for older adults, with a focus on lying, sitting, and sleeping. The project will explore different signals while following a progression of tasks chosen for their difficulty and relevance. Since the supplied recordings do not contain sleep information, we will first investigate whether a person is present in the monitored area and lying down rather than in another posture. We will then explore more detailed lying positions, such as the left or right side, back, or stomach, as well as transitions between postures. Finally, we will investigate bed occupancy and the inference of the bed's location. Sleep/wake classification and sleep-stage estimation remain longer-term objectives, depending on whether suitable data can be collected or obtained during the project.

\subsection{Goals and Metrics}
Because the intended application involves care settings where missed events may have serious consequences, Valtes wants important predictions to be made quickly. The client has specified a target of notifying a caregiver within two seconds when an event requires intervention. For these event detections, we also aim for a low false-negative rate, reducing the likelihood that a situation requiring attention goes unnoticed. These requirements motivate the investigation of machine-learning models that balance predictive performance with timely output. If bed occupancy and posture can be identified reliably, caregivers and monitored individuals could learn how long someone spends in bed, when they enter or leave it, which lying positions they prefer, and how often they get up during the night. This would provide insight into nighttime behavior.

To evaluate the success of the solutions as a whole, we will measure accuracy, F1 score, and recall on a held-out test set. Valtes provided us with three F1-score benchmarks, which could also be used with other evaluation metrics: 50\% indicates promising performance, 85\% indicates strong performance, and 95\% represents the standard for people identifying camera footage, with any solution ideally being robust to multiple people in the same room. For each objective, we will first evaluate lightweight solutions that run on the edge, then move to more computationally intensive solutions running in the cloud to establish feasibility. Only if we are unable to produce good metrics with the cloud-based model will we reevaluate our objectives and find other ways to approach the issue.

\subsection{State of the Art and Competitor Analysis}
The objectives we set out are not completely unique; existing research has been published for each desired goal in the past few years. To start with our core objective, the research provides several approaches to radar-based posture recognition. Li et al. developed ResTCN, combining residual blocks with a temporal convolutional network to classify supine, lateral, and prone positions. Using recordings from 16 volunteers, they reported an average accuracy of 82.74\% \cite{li2024sleepingpose}. Qu et al. combined overhead and side radar measurements, extracted histogram-of-oriented-gradients features, and used a support vector machine to classify four postures with 96.7\% accuracy \cite{qu2026sleep}. Their system also selected the more suitable radar view for respiratory and heart-rate monitoring based on the predicted posture. Lai et al. also tested a dual ultra-wideband radar system for sleep posture recognition \cite{lai2023dual}. These studies suggest that both the representation of radar signals and the viewing geometry influence performance. They also show that a relatively simple classifier can be a useful candidate alongside deep learning. However, their reported accuracies cannot be compared directly because the datasets, sensor arrangements, and posture categories differ. Their direct use in a commercial system is also uncertain because most of the underlying data is not publicly available and sensor differences make it difficult for results to generalize. These papers do not come from competitors; instead, they are written by machine-learning researchers interested in advancing the field.

Other researchers have also had issues with data availability for this task and have published specifically about obtaining reliable supervision. Su et al. introduced mmFree-Pose, which pairs radar point clouds with 23-joint skeletal annotations from an inertial motion-capture suit. Their dataset contains 4,534 samples from three participants, and their PointNet baseline achieved a mean localization error of 12.39 cm \cite{su2025mmfreepose}. This demonstrates an alternative to camera-based labeling, although the small participant group limits the evidence for generalization.

Even though sleep monitoring is a stretch goal, there is research that addresses these additional tasks. Krauss et al. pretrained an LSTM using movement, heart-rate variability, and respiratory features from the MESA Sleep dataset before adapting it to radar-derived features. Transfer learning improved the Matthews correlation coefficient for five-stage sleep classification from 0.25 to 0.47 \cite{krauss2025transfer}. Zhang et al. used a U-Net-based model to jointly predict oxygen saturation and detect apnea events, achieving an event-level F1 score of 0.8019 with synchronized radar and polysomnography recordings from 35 participants \cite{zhang2025apnea}. Again, these results are not directly comparable because of differences in the data and sensors, and the studies do not represent competitors in the commercial space.

One commercial alternative is Emfit, whose EMFIT QS and EMFIT CARE products use a proprietary dynamic ferro-electret sensor installed under the mattress. According to the manufacturer, EMFIT QS monitors sleep and recovery through physiological and movement signals, whereas EMFIT CARE focuses on bed occupancy and exit monitoring intended to reduce risks associated with falls and wandering \cite{emfit2026monitoring,emfit2026specifications}. This approach is limited to the bed and uses cloud processing, while radar can monitor a wider area and activities before or after bed use. Emfit does, however, operate passively without a wearable. Because these capabilities are reported by the manufacturer, they describe what the product is intended to do but do not provide an independent performance comparison with Valtes' radar.

The literature therefore supports the feasibility of several of our desired components in isolation, but does not establish their performance on Valtes' radar. Different sensors, mounting positions, preprocessing methods, and participant populations can change the input distribution. Furthermore, several posture studies use prescribed positions rather than natural overnight behavior. The biggest takeaway from the past research, beyond the methods themselves, is that building an effective model under Valtes' recording conditions might require dealing with more noise and realistic data than existing research has yet to tackle. This makes the research useful for guiding our approach but difficult to use as an objective comparison.

\subsection{Constraints and Assumptions}
Valtes provided us with limited data on posture and presence. It also does not include any data on sleep, so new data would have to be collected for an exploration into sleep detection. In the provided recordings, the available features primarily describe target distance and velocity during movement. This means that predictions about posture may need to come from the movement that got a person into that position. Therefore, the model may need to use transitional movement rather than a stable measurement of stationary posture.

With the timeline in mind, we cannot experiment indefinitely. That is why we will first focus on posture and the data we have received and later investigate whether sleep data can be recorded and used. We have access to the radar that is being used by Valtes, which means that recording our own data is a possibility.

Due to privacy concerns, it is important that only the most crucial data is stored and only for as long as needed. The environment in which the radar is used can also vary greatly depending on the room. This introduces noise that could interfere with prediction accuracy and reduce the generalizability of our solution. On the topic of generalizability, radars can have large differences in functionality. While most cameras focus only on the narrow visual band, radars can vary widely in frequency, which means that they may have an easier or more difficult time passing through certain environments. There are also different ways of sending out a signal with radar, including CW, FMCW, and pulsed radar. These factors make it difficult for data from one type of radar to be used with another. Therefore, the potential solution or model will probably only work with Valtes' radar.

Caregivers of older adults can also have varying needs. Some caregivers want to check in constantly, while others only need to look when there might be something wrong. The potential solution needs to accommodate both of these types and anything in between.

\subsection{Concept Design}
Based on the use cases described by Valtes, the goals we set out to meet those use cases, and the metrics we defined, we propose three different conceptual designs that will be part of our exploration.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.4em]
    \item Point-cloud posture classifier: preprocess radar point clouds, derive spatial and movement features, and classify presence and posture. This is the simplest design to establish a baseline.
    \item Temporal transition model: analyze sequences of radar measurements to detect movements such as sitting down, lying down, or getting out of bed. This may help when transitions are clearer than stationary postures.
    \item Video-labeled radar model: use synchronized video to create and review posture and bed-occupancy labels, train and evaluate a model using radar input, and use radar alone at prediction time. This uses video for supervision while preserving radar as the intended monitoring sensor.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Risk Assessment and Mitigation Strategies}
\begin{itemize}
    \item One issue is that the point-cloud data is really noisy and possibly sensitive to the environment. The raw data is messy and needs a lot of preprocessing. Identifying the steps needed to clean the data is a challenge, so we will compare preprocessing options and evaluate them across recording sessions.
    \item Confidentiality of the data is another risk. We should keep the repository private and not share the data with anyone outside the group. This includes the data we already have and any data that we might record in the future. Raw data should also be excluded from version control and retained only for as long as needed.
    \item The project is rather open; we risk not building a definitive product or result, but only a collection of experiments. This can be mitigated by clearly defining which experiments we will conduct and when we will move on to the next one. We will try the most pressing experiments first and make sure that they are finished before moving on.
\end{itemize}
