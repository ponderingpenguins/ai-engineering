\section{Project Formulation}

\subsection{Problem Formulation and Definition}
In our company meetings, Valtes, our primary stakeholder available for direct consultation, described its work with mmWave data as limited and exploratory. Its expectation is therefore an exploratory analysis of what can be measured, detected, and inferred from its device's recordings, and how this information could support at-home care for elderly people, with a focus on lying, sitting, and sleeping. The project will explore different signals while following a progression of tasks considered suitable in difficulty and relevance. Since the supplied recordings do not contain sleep information, we will first investigate whether a person is present in the monitored area and lying down rather than in another posture. We will then explore more detailed lying positions, on the left or right side, back, or stomach, and transitions between postures. Finally, we will investigate bed occupancy and the inference of the bed's location. Sleep/wake classification and sleep-stage estimation remain longer-term objectives, depending on the feasibility of collecting or obtaining suitable data during the project.

\subsection{Goals and Metrics}
Because the intended application involves care settings where missed events may have serious consequences, Valtes wants important predictions to be made quickly. The client has specified a target of notifying a caregiver within two seconds when an event requires intervention. For these event detections, we also aim for a low false-negative rate, reducing the likelihood that a situation requiring attention goes unnoticed. These requirements motivate the investigation of machine learning models that balance predictive performance with timely output. If bed occupancy and posture can be identified reliably, caregivers and monitored individuals could learn how long someone spends in bed, when they enter or leave it, which lying positions they prefer, and how often they get up during the night. This would provide insight into nighttime behaviour.

To evaluate the success of the solutions as a whole, we will measure metrics across accuracy, F1 and recall on a held-out test set. Valtes provided us with multiple benchmarks: 50\% means that it is good and shows some promise; 85\% means that it is really good according to Valtes; and 95\% is the standard for people identifying camera footage, with any solution ideally being robust to multiple people in the same room. For each objective, we will first evaluate lightweight solutions which run on the edge, then move to heavyweight solutions running in the cloud to ensure feasibility. Only if we are unable to produce good metrics on the heavyweight model will we reevaluate our objectives and find other ways to approach the issue.

\subsection{State of the Art and Competitor Analysis}
The objectives we lay out are not completely unique, existing research has been published across each desired goal in the past few years. To start with our core objective, the research provides several approaches to radar-based posture recognition. Li et al. developed ResTCN, combining residual blocks with a temporal convolutional network to classify supine, lateral, and prone positions. Using recordings from 16 volunteers, they reported an average accuracy of 82.74\%. Qu et al. combined overhead and side radar measurements, extracted histogram-of-oriented-gradients features, and used a support vector machine to classify four postures with 96.7\% accuracy. Their system also selected the more suitable radar view for respiratory and heart-rate monitoring based on the predicted posture. These studies suggest that both the representation of radar signals and the viewing geometry influence performance. They also show that a relatively simple classifier can be a useful candidate alongside deep learning. However, their reported accuracies cannot be compared directly because the datasets, sensor arrangements, and posture categories differ. This as well makes them unsuitable in their current state for commercial use, as most data isn't published and sensor differences make it difficult for results to generalize. These papers are not coming from competitors, but ML researchers simply interested in pushing the field forward.

Other researchers as well have had issues with data availability on the task, publishing specifically about obtaining reliable supervision. Su et al. introduced mmFree-Pose, which pairs radar point clouds with 23-joint skeletal annotations from an inertial motion-capture suit. Their dataset contains 4,534 samples from three participants, and their PointNet baseline achieved a mean localization error of 12.39 cm. This demonstrates an alternative to camera-based labeling, although the small participant group limits the evidence for generalization.

Even if the task of sleep monitoring is a stretch goal, there exists research which addresses these additional tasks. Krauss et al. pretrained an LSTM using movement, heart-rate variability, and respiratory features from the MESA Sleep dataset before adapting it to radar-derived features. Transfer learning improved the Matthews correlation coefficient for five-stage sleep classification from 0.25 to 0.47. Zhang et al. used a U-Net-based model to jointly predict oxygen saturation and detect apnea events, achieving an event-level F1-score of 0.8019 with synchronized radar and polysomnography recordings from 35 participants. Again, these results were not comparable due to data and sensor difference, nor are they a competitor in the commercial space.

The literature therefore supports the feasibility of several of our desired components in isolation, but does not establish their performance on Valtes' radar. Different sensors, mounting positions, preprocessing methods, and participant populations can change the input distribution. Furthermore, several posture studies use prescribed positions rather than natural overnight behaviour. This is hopefully the biggest take away from the past research beyond methods, that building an effective model under Valtes' recording conditions might require dealing with more noise and realistic data than existing research has yet to tackle, which makes the research useful for learning, but difficult to use as an objective comparison.

\subsection{Constraints and Assumptions}
Valtes provided us with limited data on posture and presence. It also does not include any data on sleep, so for an exploration into sleep detection new data would have to be collected. Valtes' radar also can only detect distance and velocity from a moving target, which means that any predictions about posture must come out of the movement that got a person into that position. Therefore the model does not detect stationary posture specifically but it uses the transitional movement to determine the final posture.

With the timeline in mind, we can not endlessly experiment. That is why we will first focus on posture and the data we have received and later look into if sleep data is possible to record and use. We do have access to the radar that is being used by Valtes, which means that recording our own data is not a constraint.

Due to privacy reasons it is important that only the most crucial data is stored and only for as long as needed. The environment that the radar would be used in can also vary a lot depending on the room. This introduces a lot of noise that could interfere with the prediction accuracy and reduce the amount of generalizability our solution could provide. On the topic of generalizability, radars can have large differences in functionality. While most cameras only focus on the narrow visual band, radars can wildly differ in frequency which means that it could have an easier or more difficult time to get through certain environments. There are also different ways of sending out a signal with radars (CW, FMCW or Pulsed). These two facts make it difficult for data of one type of radar to be used with other types of radar. Therefore the potential solution or model will probably only be able to work on Valtes' radar.

The caretakers of the elderly can also have varying needs. Some caretakers want to constantly check in, while others only have to look when there might be something wrong. The potential solution needs to accommodate both of these types and anywhere in between.

\subsection{Concept Design}
Based on use cases described by Valtes, the goals we set out to meet that use case, and the metrics we defined, we propose 3 different conceptual designs which will be part of our exploration.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Point-cloud posture classifier: preprocess radar point clouds, derive spatial and movement features, and classify presence and posture. This is the simplest design to establish a baseline.
    \item Temporal transition model: analyse sequences of radar measurements to detect movements such as sitting down, lying down, or getting out of bed. This may help when transitions are clearer than stationary postures.
    \item Video-labelled radar model: use synchronized video to create and review posture labels, train and evaluate a model using radar input, and use radar alone at prediction time. This design uses video for supervision while preserving radar as the intended monitoring sensor.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Risk Assessment and Mitigation Strategies}
\begin{itemize}
    \item One issue is that the point cloud data is really noisy and possibly sensitive to the environment. The raw data is messy and needs a lot of preprocessing. Identifying what steps need to be taken to clean the data is a challenge.
    \item Confidentiality of the data. We should keep the repository private and not share the data with anyone outside of the group. This includes the data we already have and any data that we might record in the future.
    \item The project is rather open, we risk not building a definitive product or result, just collections of experiments. But this can be mitigated by clearly defining what experiments we do and when we would move on to the next experiment. This means that we would try the most pressing experiments first and make sure that they are finished before we move on to the next.
\end{itemize}
